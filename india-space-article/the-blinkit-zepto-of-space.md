# The Blinkit & Zepto of Space: India Now Delivers to Orbit in 15 Minutes

*Alt titles: "Rocket Singh 2.0: India's Space Startups Are Eating the Launch Market" • "10-Minute Delivery Was Cute. Try 450 km, Straight Up."*

---

This morning, an Indian startup delivered six packages to an address 450 kilometres straight up. Delivery time from liftoff: about 15 minutes. Not even your quick-commerce app can beat that.

At Sriharikota on July 18, Skyroot Aerospace's Vikram-1 became the first privately built Indian rocket to reach orbit. Mission Aagaman — "the arrival" — held its breath through a 35-minute delay after a technical niggle at T-minus 5, then lifted off and dropped six payloads, including two satellites, into a 450 km low Earth orbit. With that, India became only the third country on Earth — after the US and China — where a private company has put something into orbit.

And just like quick commerce, this story isn't about one player. It's about a duo. Skyroot and Agnikul Cosmos are the Blinkit and Zepto of space tech: two hungry startups, chasing the same market, taking completely different routes — and together dragging an entire country's industry into a new era.

(Never heard of Blinkit or Zepto? They're India's 10-minute grocery delivery apps — two rivals who convinced a billion people that waiting half an hour for milk is a human rights violation. Same idea here, just with a longer ladder: impatience as a business model, now pointed at the sky.)

Here's why this duo, and the 300+ startups behind them, might just win the global space game.

## First, what actually happened today

Vikram-1 is a small rocket by global standards — four stages, the first three burning solid fuel, topped by a liquid-fuelled kick stage for precise satellite drop-offs. It can carry roughly 300–480 kg to low Earth orbit. Its party trick isn't size. It's speed and convenience: an all-carbon-fibre body, and a design meant to be assembled and launched within 24–72 hours of a customer saying "go."

Skyroot's founders — ex-ISRO engineers Pawan Chandana and Naga Bharath Daka — call it the "Uber to space." You don't wait months for a seat on someone else's giant rocket. You book the whole cab.

Investors bought the pitch before the rocket even flew. In May, Skyroot raised $60 million at a $1.1 billion valuation, becoming India's first spacetech unicorn, with about $160 million raised in total. Today, the product demo happened 450 km above the Bay of Bengal.

## Meet the other half of the duo

If Skyroot is the operations machine, Agnikul Cosmos is the mad-scientist sibling from IIT Madras.

Agnikul's entire rocket engine — the Agnilet — is 3D-printed as a single piece of metal. One print job. Zero assembly. What traditionally takes hundreds of parts and months of welding, Agnikul prints in about a week. In May 2024, their Agnibaan SOrTeD test flight made it the first single-piece 3D-printed engine ever flown, anywhere — launched from India's first private launchpad, which Agnikul built themselves.

Their rocket, Agnibaan, is designed like a thali: configurable. Need to launch 30 kg? Plug in fewer engines. Need 300 kg? Add more. Print engines on demand, roll out the mobile launchpad (named Dhanush), launch from almost anywhere.

The roadmap gets bolder. Agnikul has test-fired four engines clustered together, is targeting its first orbital flight later this year, and its Mission 02 aims for something India has never done: recovering an orbital-class rocket booster — the first step toward reusability — plus an upper stage that stays alive in orbit as a platform instead of becoming junk. Former ISRO chairman Dr. S. Somanath just joined their board. The company is valued around $500 million, with $75+ million raised.

Two startups. One prints rockets like documents. The other launches them like cab rides. That's the duopoly energy this market needed.

## How the giants did it — the honest version

To see why India's route is special, look at how the US and China got here.

**The American way: burn money until it works.** SpaceX is a genuine marvel — but it grew up on NASA's chequebook, with billions in government contracts, and it still took six years and three failed launches before reaching orbit. Twenty years later the result is spectacular: Falcon 9 boosters land themselves and fly again, and a dedicated Falcon 9 launch (~$74M a ticket) works out to roughly $2,900 per kg. But that's a bus service — brilliant if you're going where the bus goes, on the bus's schedule. Want your own small rocket, your own orbit, your own timing? America's option is Rocket Lab's Electron at about $25,000 per kg. Nearly nine times more.

**The Chinese way: the state plays startup.** China's "private" space firms — LandSpace, iSpace and friends — are impressive and heavily state-enabled. LandSpace flew the world's first methane-fuelled rocket to orbit in 2023, and its reusable Zhuque-3 reached orbit last December, though the booster crashed while trying to land. They plan to build 10 rockets and 100 engines this year and attempt a re-flight by Q4. Serious pace. But it's scale bought with state muscle — and geopolitics means half the world's satellite customers can't or won't fly on a Chinese rocket anyway.

## The gap in the middle — and why India fits it perfectly

Look at that chart again. Between the $7,000/kg shared bus and the $25,000/kg private cab sits a huge, underserved market: thousands of small satellites that want *dedicated* launches — their own orbit, their own schedule — without the luxury-taxi bill.

That's the lane India's duo is built for. And India brings three unfair advantages to it:

**1. Frugality is a genetic trait, not a strategy.** This is the country that sent a probe to Mars for $74 million — less than the budget of the movie *Gravity* — and soft-landed Chandrayaan-3 on the Moon for about the same. Indian engineering culture doesn't treat "cheap" and "excellent" as opposites; it was never given permission to. Skyroot and Agnikul hired straight from this culture, often from ISRO itself.

**2. The government switched from gatekeeper to launchpad.** Until 2020, private companies literally weren't allowed to build launch vehicles in India. Then the sector was opened, IN-SPACe was created as a single-window regulator, and the 2023 Space Policy locked in the rules. Startups now use ISRO's test facilities, expertise, and Sriharikota's launch pads — decades of taxpayer-built infrastructure, available instead of duplicated. The government even subsidises private launches by 30%, up to $3,000 per kg. It took SpaceX six years to reach orbit with NASA's help; Skyroot did it in about eight from founding, on a fraction of the money — India's entire spacetech startup ecosystem has raised less than SpaceX often collects in a single funding round.

**3. Speed as a product.** A rocket that integrates in 24 hours (Skyroot) and an engine that prints in a week (Agnikul) means launch-on-demand isn't a slogan — it's the actual spec sheet. When a satellite operator loses a bird and needs a replacement up *now*, neither the Falcon 9 bus queue nor a hand-welded rocket helps. A printed, truck-mounted, launch-anywhere rocket does.

## The scoreboard

India's space economy is about $8.4 billion today — roughly 2% of the global pie. The target: $44 billion and an 8–10% share by 2033. Over 300 startups are now building everything from launch vehicles to hyperspectral imaging satellites (hello, Pixxel), backed by a growing pool of domestic and global capital — Singapore's GIC and Temasek are already on Skyroot's cap table.

Let's be honest about the caveats, because fans of this sector should be its toughest critics. One successful launch is a milestone, not a market. Cadence — launching again, and again, on schedule — is what converts headlines into revenue. Reusability, the single biggest cost lever, is still ahead of both Indian players while SpaceX lands boosters for breakfast. And the funding gap with the US and China is real.

But direction matters more than position. The US proved private space works, with the world's deepest pockets. China is proving the state can brute-force it. India is proving something more interesting: that you can do it *frugally* — with printed engines, borrowed-not-duplicated infrastructure, subsidised launch economics, and engineers who treat a tight budget as a design constraint, not an excuse.

The giants built the space equivalent of container shipping and luxury couriers. India is building the hyperlocal delivery network — small, fast, cheap, everywhere, on demand.

This morning the first delivery arrived. Fifteen minutes, doorstep to orbit. The app is live, the riders are ready — and the tips, for once, go to Indian engineering.
